Poland Macroeconomic Time Series Analysis¶

Reference Paper:¶

This project is based on the following academic study: "Interactions between Output, Inflation, Interest Rates and House Prices: International Evidence".

We reproduce the VAR (Vector Autoregression) methodology from this paper and apply it to Poland's macroeconomic data.

Reference Link: [Access the paper here] https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09599916.2010.499015

Objective¶

To analyze the relationship between:

  • Inflation
  • Interest Rates
  • Housing Prices

Interpretation¶

The three variables clearly show how key parts of the economy are connected and influence each other over time.

  • Inflation remained relatively stable in the early period (2010–2013), followed by a phase of very low or near-zero inflation between 2014 and 2016. From 2017 onwards, inflation gradually increased, reaching a significant peak around 2022–2023. This spike likely reflects macroeconomic shocks such as global inflationary pressures. After 2023, inflation declines, indicating stabilization.

  • Interest rates show a decreasing trend from 2010 to 2020, reaching near-zero levels around 2020. This suggests an accommodative monetary policy. However, around 2021–2022, interest rates increase sharply, likely as a response to rising inflation. After 2023, interest rates gradually decrease again.

  • Housing prices show a steady upward trend in the long run. This growth becomes even more noticeable during periods of low interest rates, such as between 2013 and 2020, when borrowing is cheaper and demand for housing is stronger.

  • Overall, the visual patterns suggest a potential relationship between interest rates, inflation, and housing prices. However, further time-series analysis is required to formally assess these interactions.

  • The observed co-movements among the variables motivate the use of a VAR model to examine their dynamic interactions more formally

ADF Test Interpretation¶

  • We applied the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test to check whether the variables are stationary over time.
  • Since housing prices are measured as an index, we also tested the logarithm of the housing price series.
  • The results show that all three variables — inflation, interest rate, and ln(housing price) — have p-values greater than 0.05.
  • This means that the variables are not stationary, and their statistical properties (such as mean and variance) change over time.
  • In simple terms, these variables follow trends and are influenced by long-term economic movements, which is common for macroeconomic data.
  • Because the data is non-stationary, it is not suitable for time series modeling in its original form.
  • Therefore, we apply differencing to remove trends and stabilize the data before building the VAR model.

The VAR framework allows each variable to be explained by its own past values as well as the past values of the other variables, making it suitable for studying the interactions between inflation, interest rates, and housing prices.

VAR Model Results and Discussion¶

The lag selection results suggest that a VAR(4) model is the most suitable choice, as it provides the lowest AIC and FPE values. This indicates that the relationships between inflation, interest rates, and housing prices tend to develop over roughly four quarters.

Looking at the housing price equation, there is clear evidence of persistence. Housing prices in the previous quarter have a positive and significant effect on current prices, meaning that trends in the housing market tend to continue rather than change quickly. In simple terms, if prices were rising in the last quarter, they are likely to keep rising in the current one.

Inflation also exerts a delayed influence on housing prices. The coefficient of the fourth lag of inflation is positive and statistically significant (coefficient = 0.0043, p = 0.024). This result implies that inflationary pressures may take up to one year to be transmitted into the housing market. Rising inflation can increase construction costs, replacement costs, and inflation expectations, which may ultimately contribute to higher housing prices.

On the other hand, interest-rate coefficients in the housing-price equation are statistically significant at the 5% level. For example, the first lag of interest rates has a coefficient of 0.0025 (p = 0.533), while the fourth lag has a coefficient of −0.0033 (p = 0.438). These results suggest that short-run fluctuations in interest rates.

The inflation equation indicates substantial inflation persistence. The first lag of inflation is highly significant, with a coefficient of 0.422 (p = 0.004), while the fourth lag remains statistically significant and negative (coefficient = −0.361, p = 0.015). This suggests that inflation dynamics are strongly influenced by their own historical values.

Overall, the results suggest that the housing market in Poland is mainly driven by its own past behavior, along with delayed effects from inflation. Interest rates, however, seem to have a weaker short-term impact within this period.

Impulse Response Analysis¶

While the VAR(4) results show relationships between inflation, interest rates, and housing prices, the coefficients alone do not fully explain how shocks move through the system over time. To better understand this, we use Impulse Response Functions (IRFs), which show how a sudden change in one variable affects the others in the following quarters. This helps us clearly see the direction, strength, and duration of these effects in the Polish economy.

Impulse Response Function (IRF) Results¶

  • To better understand how shocks affect the system, we estimated impulse response functions (IRFs) over a 12-quarter period.

  • The results show that an inflation shock has a delayed effect on housing prices. Prices may fall slightly at first, but after about a year they start to rise and remain positive for several quarters. This supports the idea that inflation affects the housing market with a lag, likely through higher costs and changing expectations.

  • An interest-rate shock, on the other hand, leads to a decline in housing prices. The negative effect is strongest after about 4–5 quarters before gradually fading. Even though interest rates were not significant in the VAR results, this suggests they still influence housing through indirect or delayed channels.

  • Housing prices also react strongly to their own shocks. A positive shock increases prices immediately, and the effect remains persistent over time, confirming strong momentum in the housing market.

  • Overall, both inflation and interest rates show gradual adjustment rather than immediate effects. In general, housing prices respond to macroeconomic shocks with delays, and the effects tend to fade over time rather than being permanent.

Interpretation of Granger Causality Test:¶

Granger Causality Hypothesis:¶

The Granger causality test is used to examine whether one time series can predict another. The hypothesis are defined as follows:

  • Null Hypothesis (H₀): The independent variable does not Granger-cause the dependent variable.
  • Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): The independent variable Granger-causes the dependent variable.

The decision rule is based on the p-value:

  • If p-value < 0.05 → Reject H₀ → Causality exists
  • If p-value ≥ 0.05 → Fail to reject H₀ → No causality

The results indicate that inflation Granger-causes housing prices (p = 0.0378), suggesting that past inflation contains significant predictive information for future movements in housing prices. This finding supports the VAR and impulse response analyses, which showed that inflation shocks have a delayed positive effect on housing prices.

In contrast, interest rates do not Granger-cause housing prices (p = 0.3915), indicating that past interest rate movements do not significantly improve the prediction of housing price changes within the sample period.

The tests also show that interest rates Granger-cause inflation (p = 0.0162), implying that monetary policy conditions contain useful information for forecasting future inflation. Meanwhile, the evidence that inflation Granger-causes interest rates is weaker (p = 0.0614), although it remains marginally significant at the 10% level.

Overall, the results suggest that inflation plays an important role in predicting housing prices, while the relationship between inflation and interest rates appears to be bidirectional but stronger from interest rates to inflation.

Conclusions & Final Comments¶

1. Model Summary & Technical Validation¶

  • Stationarity & Sizing: The Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) tests confirmed that the raw macro series were non-stationary. Applying first-order differencing successfully stabilized the series. Based on AIC criteria, a lag order of 4 months/quarters was utilized for the Vector Autoregression (VAR) framework.

  • The Granger Causality Test: While individual lag coefficients in the VAR output suggested localized relationships (such as a delayed impact from inflation), the joint Granger Causality Test is the ultimate arbiter of predictability. Because all calculated $p$-values exceeded the 5% significance threshold ($p \ge 0.05$), we strictly fail to reject the null hypothesis ($H_0$). Econometrically, past values of inflation and interest rates do not provide statistically significant predictive power for Polish housing prices within this linear model.

2. Methodological Limitations (Why Causality Was Not Found)¶

The study failed to find the Granger-Causal relationship.This points to three distinct structural constraints:

  • Temporal Alignment Bias: Forward-filling the quarterly housing price index to match a monthly frequency introduced artificial step-patterns (data plateaus) into the dependent variable. This smoothing mechanism inherently dampens the mathematical covariance required for lag-based causality tests to succeed.

  • Macroeconomic Regimes (2010–2025): The dataset spans unprecedented macroeconomic disruptions in Poland, including the post-pandemic recovery and regional geopolitical shifts. Standard linear VAR models frequently lose predictive stability when subjected to such extreme structural breaks.

  • Omitted Structural Variables: The Polish residential real estate market during this era was heavily influenced by forces exogenous to our three-variable system—specifically rapid nominal wage growth, massive demographic shifts, and targeted government home-buyer subsidy programs.

3. Recommendations for Future Research¶

To build upon this reproduced baseline, future iterations should:

  1. Apply Temporal Disaggregation: Utilize advanced distribution techniques (like the Chow-Lin method) instead of basic forward-filling to interpolate monthly housing values using high-frequency indicators like construction permits.

  2. Shift to VECM: Test for cointegration. If a long-run equilibrium relationship exists among the raw levels, a Vector Error Correction Model (VECM) should be deployed to capture long-term causal trajectories without losing information to first-differencing.
